# Krumpy's writing voice

This is an evidence-based starting point, inferred from first-person notes, scope documents and user prompts. It describes writing choices, not personality or beliefs. Its two primary anchors are the Ultimate Eternum S2 Scope Summary, confirmed by Krumpy as almost entirely his writing, and Krumpy’s Eternum Ramblings. The Scope anchors formal structure and mechanical explanation; the Ramblings anchor candid discussion. Krumpy confirmed the other supplied attachments may have AI influence, so their wording carries less weight. See [sources.md](sources.md).

## The underlying approach

**Begin with something people experience.** Explain what is happening, why it causes a problem, and whom it affects. Prefer the cost in someone's time, choices, effort or incentives over a vague claim that a system needs improvement.

**Reason through the mechanism.** The characteristic move is from an observed problem to a proposal, then through its rules to its likely effects. A reader should be able to challenge the logic. Retain the intermediate step: how does the proposed change produce that outcome?

**Consider the person on the other side of the change.** Discuss who benefits, who pays, what becomes harder, and how a rule might be exploited. Balance participation and accessibility against the value of commitment or expertise when the subject calls for it. Do not invent an upside/downside pair for every paragraph.

**Own a judgement, bound the certainty.** When the brief supplies a view, say it plainly. Use “I think”, “I’d like to see”, or “my proposal” when they accurately convey the author's position. Keep uncertainty about feasibility, unmeasured effects and open choices. Do not turn a direct opinion into anonymous consensus, or a tentative idea into a commitment.

**Name the actor and owner when it matters to the argument.** In proposals, make clear whether the team, organisation, customer or another participant acts, owns an asset, receives funding or makes the decision. Use “we” and “you” naturally when the referent is clear; replace them when they blur responsibility. Explain where money or resources go when the document depends on that flow.

**Keep the stakes proportionate.** Describe the actual consequence of a decision. Do not portray a revisable proposal as a final chance or imply failure is permanent when the source leaves room for iteration.

**Make the idea testable.** Use an example, sequence, calculation or comparison when it clarifies the proposal. Numbers must come from the task's sources or be explicitly hypothetical. An example shows the mechanism; it does not establish that the proposal will succeed.

**Keep the conversation open where it is open.** End a discussion with the actual unresolved question or next useful decision, if there is one. A reference page can simply end after its last useful fact. Avoid a ceremonial conclusion that repeats the opening.

## Professional voice: default

Direct, practical and engaged. Use complete paragraphs with enough explanation to follow the reasoning. A short sentence can state the position; a longer one can work through the qualification. Use familiar connectors such as “because”, “so”, “while”, “however” and “for example” when they express a real relationship. Do not sprinkle them mechanically.

Keep terms stable. Repeating “proposal”, “player”, “cost” or a named system is better than cycling through approximate synonyms. Define an unfamiliar term when the reader needs it. Domain vocabulary can be precise; do not replace it just to sound less technical.

Use headings, bullets and tables to make a long document usable. The scope samples use Problem / Solution / Requirements / Expected result effectively. Use that structure when it fits a scope, not as a compulsory template for an email or essay. Separate confirmed requirements from options and questions.

British/Australian spellings such as “organised”, “behaviour” and “randomised” are a modest default supported by the strongest sample, not an identity inference. Preserve the destination's conventions, exact technical names and quoted text. The game resource named “Labor”, for example, is not a spelling error to normalise.

## Candid discussion: when the brief calls for it

Allow stronger first-person judgements, conversational questions, contractions and an occasional dry aside. Keep existing humour when it makes the author's point. The source can be blunt, but bluntness does not require insults or profanity. Do not add either as a signature. Keep an existing swear word only if it suits the requested audience and degree of editing.

Do not reproduce personal stories from the calibration corpus in a new piece. If the current draft contains a genuine anecdote, it can stay. A hypothetical example should be presented as hypothetical, not as something the author did.

## Documentation and explanations

Let the mechanism lead. State the applicable rule, actor, action, condition and exception. Explain the user-facing consequence. Use the second person when giving the reader an instruction; use the relevant role when describing a system. Do not force every sentence to say “you”.

A little thematic language can fit game documentation, but it must not bury the rule. Outside a game context, carry over the clear reasoning and practical perspective, not Realms terminology or fantasy imagery.

## What not to imitate

- Rough-draft typos, ambiguous pronouns, run-on sentences or outdated facts.
- The wording of meeting summaries as if every participant were Krumpy.
- Promotional passages or team-authored content merely because it lives in his repository.
- Repeated phrases from assistant-written drafts, commit messages or task summaries as evidence of his prose.
- A cartoon of a blunt gamer: manufactured swearing, jokes, contrarianism, or “I think” in every paragraph.

Good voice matching preserves the author's way of reasoning. Surface quirks are secondary.
